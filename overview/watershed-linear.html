The idea

Why a watershed

A watershed is all the land that drains to one river. What happens upstream, where the rain falls and what the ground holds, decides what reaches the river downstream.

Learning design works the same way. Decisions made early, about who a resource is for and what it assumes, decide who can act on it later. Thousands of people already work on each part of this. My practice is one contribution among them, and this page shows where it joins.

The one problem

What is known does not reliably become what people can do.

Four measures show the size of the gap, in health and in education, worldwide: how many people are reached, how good the care is when they are, how many children learn the basics, and how long research takes to reach practice.

4.5 billion people, more than half the world's population, were not fully covered by essential health services in 2021

What it measures
Coverage of essential health services, tracked by WHO and the World Bank. Progress slowed after 2015, with no improvement from 2019 to 2021.
What it does not show
Whether the care people did receive was of good quality.

5 million deaths a year in 137 low- and middle-income countries from poor-quality care, against 3.6 million from not using care at all

What it measures
Deaths in 2016 from 61 conditions that health care can treat, modelled from coverage and quality data. Where care was received, its quality decided more deaths than access did.
What it does not show
A count. The estimate rests on modelled quality, and the authors give wide uncertainty ranges.

70% of ten-year-olds in low- and middle-income countries could not read and understand a simple text in 2022, up from 57% before the pandemic

What it measures
Learning poverty, a joint estimate by the World Bank, UNESCO and UNICEF that combines children out of school with children in school who fall below a minimum reading level.
What it does not show
High-income countries, or subjects other than reading. It is a simulation for 2022, not a survey.

About 17 years from research to practice: a figure several studies reached while measuring different stages of the process

What it measures
A review of 23 papers that quantified time lags in health research. Few were comparable, because they measured different things at different points.
What it does not show
A single measured average. The authors caution that the agreement on 17 years hides important differences.

Read together, the figures say something more precise than that a gap exists. Poor-quality care now causes more deaths than lack of access. Most ten-year-olds in low- and middle-income countries cannot read a simple text. And the research that could change both takes a generation to arrive. The gap is not mainly a shortage of knowledge. It sits between knowledge and practice: in how evidence is turned into something people can use, who can use it, who carries it, and whether anyone finds out if it worked. That is the ground learning design works on.

The selection

Why these five

Many problems are large. These five were chosen because each passes three tests at once, and because they depend on each other.

Each is large: the people affected number in the hundreds of millions or more, across countries at every income level. Each is neglected in a specific way: not for lack of attention, but because the part that decides the outcome, the design of what practitioners actually receive, is rarely studied or resourced as carefully as the evidence or the policy around it. And each is tractable: in at least one rigorous trial, careful design moved the result.

They are also one system. Evidence that arrives without a plan for use is lost at the source. Materials that depend on a request miss the people least likely to ask. Workers trained without supervision lose skills in routine care. Programmes never evaluated by what people do afterwards cannot teach the field anything. A gain in one place is lost in the next unless the next is designed too, which is why the cases are read as one watershed and not five problems.

The fifth is different in kind. Generative AI changes the conditions for the other four at once: how evidence is found and summarised, how accessible formats are made, how practitioners are trained, and whether assessment can still show what a person can do. It is the climate the other four work in.

The map

Five tributaries, one river

Read in order, the stages follow the water. Each stage flows into the next. What stage 4 finds returns to stage 1. Stage 5 affects every stage at once.

  1. Stage 1, the source: Getting evidence into practice. Research becomes something people can use where they work.
  2. Stage 2, who can use it: Access that does not depend on asking. Learning works for disabled people from the first draft, without depending on a request.
  3. Stage 3, who carries it: Preparing the people who carry care. Community health workers and other non-specialist providers are prepared, and supported after training.
  4. Stage 4, feedback: Knowing whether it worked. Organizations learn which programs change practice. What they learn returns to stage 1.
  5. Stage 5, the climate: Learning through the generative AI transition. Changes how people learn and are assessed at every stage at once.

The river: People able to act on what is known. Where the four stages lead. Each stage decides how much is lost on the way.

How each case is read

Three questions, asked of every case

Each question is asked twice: what is, and what could be. Scale asks how many people a problem affects, and how much. Neglectedness asks how many resources already go to it, and where one more contributor would add most. Solvability asks how much of what remains more effort would solve, and what it would take.

Scale

  • What is: How many people does it affect, and how much?
  • What could be: If it were solved, how much better would things be, and for how many people?

Neglectedness

  • What is: How many resources already go to it?
  • What could be: Where would one more contributor add most?

Solvability

  • What is: How much of what remains would more effort solve?
  • What could be: What would it take?

Two further sections follow in every case. Where effort counts for less, and for more, separates the parts of a problem that are already well served, or that design cannot move, from the parts where one more well-placed contributor changes the result. What design cannot do alone names the conditions held by partners: funded posts, devices and connectivity, time, supplies and management support. Each case ends with its open questions, so that what is uncertain is as visible as what is known.

The theory of change

How the five connect

If

learning is designed with all five of these conditions:

  1. Learning is designed from synthesised evidence and local barriers.
    Assumes: systematic reviews, not single studies, are what transfers, and local barriers are diagnosed before teaching begins.
  2. Access is built in from the first draft, with disabled people leading.
    Assumes: learners' devices and assistive technology can use what is built, and those who need built-in features use them.
  3. Support continues after training.
    Assumes: funded roles and supplies exist for trained workers, and supervision is resourced alongside training.
  4. Learning is evaluated by what changes in practice.
    Assumes: evaluation findings reach the people who can act on them, and they act.
  5. Learning is assessed in ways that stay valid as AI changes how people learn.
    Assumes: learning gains seen in short trials last, and access to tools is fair across learners.

Then

more people can act on what is known, more of the time,

And

the field learns faster which designs work, because what evaluation finds returns to the evidence.

This holds only where conditions outside design are in place or held by partners: funded posts, devices and connectivity, time, supplies and management support. It describes a contribution, not a sole cause.

The conditions are joined, which is why the theory is stated as one chain and not five. Synthesised evidence reaches no one if the materials made from it are inaccessible. Accessible materials change nothing if the people who deliver care are not supported to use them. Supported practitioners cannot improve a programme that is never evaluated by what they do. Evaluation that stays inside one organization teaches the field nothing, so the next design starts from the same place. Each link is an assumption that can fail, and each case names its weakest one.

The limits

What the evidence does not yet show

Three limits run through all five cases, and are stated here once.

The strongest evidence comes from health care, where trials of implementation and training are most common. The method is not specific to health, and the cases include education, workforce and higher-education examples, but whether effects of the same size hold in other sectors is an open question in every case.

The trials are short. The AI tutoring studies ran from two lessons to six weeks; the community health worker trials followed people for six months. Whether gains last, and whether they survive the move from a trial to routine services that offer less support, is rarely measured.

Cost is rarely reported. Choosing between designs depends on what each costs per person reached or per practice changed, and most of the sources cited do not say.

My practice

How I know it is working

The work is designed against six levels.

Reach
Did it reach the people the default usually misses? Example: accessible formats available at release, not on request.
Adoption
Are others choosing to use or adapt it? Example: known adaptations of a published resource or method.
Practice
Did what people do change, checked at 60 to 90 days? Example: a follow-up on behaviour, not satisfaction.
Maintenance
Is it kept current? Example: a named owner and a scheduled update cycle.
Embedding
Is it in a template, procedure or standard? Example: part of onboarding, a course template or a written procedure.
Field
Is it being cited, critiqued or built on? Example: citations, published critiques, or contributions to a standard.
