Access that does not depend on asking

How can learning work for disabled people without depending on a request?

What solving it would make possible

Materials that disabled people can use on the same day as everyone else, without disclosing anything. Accommodation work concentrates on needs that are genuinely individual. Features built for some serve many: in one national survey, students used captions to focus and retain information, though fewer than one in five reported difficulty hearing.

The three questions

Scale

What is:

  • About 1.3 billion people, 16% of the world's population, live with significant disability.
  • About one third of first-year Canadian university students in 2025 reported a disability or impairment, most commonly related to mental health.

What could be: access built in once serves every learner who meets the resource.

Neglectedness

What is:

  • In a US national cohort, 28% of college students who had received special education in high school told their college they had a disability, and 19% received accommodations, against 87% in high school.
  • In February 2026, 95.9% of the top million home pages had detected WCAG 2 failures, up from 94.8% in 2025.

What could be: the gap sits where materials are made, which is where design acts.

Solvability

What is:

  • Supports open to all students, such as writing and maths centres, were linked to persistence for students with disabilities.
  • Across more than 100 studies, captions improved comprehension of video for viewers in general, not only deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers.
  • Evidence for UDL as a whole framework is contested; the studies cited for it mostly support single techniques.

What could be: techniques with the strongest evidence become defaults.

Reading the figures

1.3 billion, 16%
What it measures: A global estimate of significant disability
What it does not show: A precise count
One third of first-year students
What it measures: Self-report in a survey of over 11,000 students at 39 universities
What it does not show: All Canadian students, or students at colleges
28% told their college
What it measures: A US national cohort who had received special education in high school, followed up to eight years after leaving
What it does not show: Students never identified in school, or students today
95.9% of home pages
What it measures: Errors an automated tool detects on one home page per site
What it does not show: Problems that need human judgement
UDL effect sizes
What it measures: Mostly one-group, before-and-after designs
What it does not show: That UDL caused the gain

Theory of change

  1. Inputs: leadership by disabled people; WCAG, UDL and disability studies. Assumes designers have time, mandate and budget.
  2. Activities: captions, transcripts, flexible formats and deadlines in the first draft. Assumes learners' devices and assistive technology can use them.
  3. Outputs: resources people can use without asking. Assumes those who need built-in features use them.
  4. Outcomes: more disabled learners take part and persist without needing to ask. Assumes universal supports aid persistence.
  5. Impact: learning reaches people it now misses, and accommodation covers the rest. Assumes learners are in school and connected.

The weakest link is 3 to 4: the sources reviewed here did not measure whether built-in access reduces requests.

Evidence from the field

Accessible digital textbooks, Nicaragua

Prototypes with narration, sign language video and audio description were tested with 113 first- and second-grade students, 29 of them disabled, in three schools. Most students were engaged, and disabled students' independence grew. The prototypes worked least well for students with visual disabilities, most of whom had not yet been taught a screen reader. The authors note the sample is too small to generalise by disability type.

Captions and transcripts, United States

A survey of more than 2,100 students at 15 institutions found students used captions to focus, retain information and cope with poor audio, and used transcripts to search and study. Nearly one in five reported some difficulty hearing. Learning gains were not measured.

Who leads this work

Disabled people and disability-led organisations, alongside accessibility advisors and accommodation professionals, CAST, the Inclusive Design Research Centre at OCAD University, and UNICEF's Accessible Digital Textbooks for All. Design joins where materials are made.

What design cannot do alone

  • Individual accommodation. Built-in access reduces how often it is needed and never replaces it. Held by accessibility advisors and accommodation offices.
  • Infrastructure. Devices, connectivity and assistive technology training shaped outcomes in the field evidence. Held by institutions and ministries.
  • School access. A resource cannot reach a child who is not in school. Across 42 countries, children with disabilities were 49% more likely never to have attended school.

Open questions

  • Does access built in from the start reduce accommodation requests?
  • Do features tested in small pilots hold across an institution's whole course inventory?
  • Are AI-generated accessible formats reliable without expert review? Early tests found failures on complex STEM diagrams.
