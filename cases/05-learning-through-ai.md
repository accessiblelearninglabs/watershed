# Learning through the generative AI transition

How can assessment still show what a person can do?

---

## What solving it would make possible

AI used in ways that build learning people keep, with credentials that stay fair and valid for every learner. Design decides which way it goes: in one trial, a tutor with guardrails raised practice grades by 127% and largely avoided the harm an unrestricted chatbot caused to later unaided performance.¹

---

## The three questions

**Scale**
- 94% of UK full-time undergraduates use generative AI to help with assessed work, up from 89% in 2025.²
- AI detectors misclassified essays by non-native English writers as AI-written 61% of the time, while essays by US eighth-graders were classified almost perfectly.³

*What could be:* assessment that stays valid for every student, whatever tools they use.

**Neglectedness**
- Only 23% of UK higher education teaching staff said AI training was available to them, while 37% had already brought AI into their teaching.⁴
- Policy activity is substantial: the OECD describes five emerging policy areas for generative AI in higher education.⁵

*What could be:* the gap between policy and teaching practice is where design acts.

**Solvability**
- An unrestricted chatbot raised practice grades by 48% but lowered later unaided scores by 17%. A tutor with guardrails raised practice grades by 127% and largely avoided the harm.¹
- A carefully built AI tutor produced learning gains of 0.63 standard deviations over in-class active learning, across two lessons.⁶

*What could be:* the same technology helps or harms depending on how it is designed into learning.

---

## Reading the figures

| Figure | Measures | Does not show |
|---|---|---|
| 94% use AI for assessed work | Self-report from UK full-time undergraduates in one survey; 12% report putting AI-generated text into assessed work² | Use for every task, or how often |
| Detector misclassification, 61% | Seven detectors, about 90 essays per group, mostly older tools³ | Whether current detectors share the bias. The direction of the finding is the durable part |
| 0.63 standard deviations | Two physics lessons, with expert prompts and pre-written solutions⁶ | Retention, or results on other content |

---

## Theory of change

1. **Inputs:** staff time, AI literacy training, and program-level assessment maps. *Assumes training reaches staff.*⁴
2. **Activities:** redesign assessment by program, with a few secure points and openly AI-supported tasks elsewhere. *Assumes detection cannot carry validity, so design must.*³ ⁷
3. **Outputs:** clear AI rules per task, tutors with guardrails, and reviewed accessible media. *Assumes guardrails prevent the harm seen with unrestricted chatbots.*¹
4. **Outcomes:** students learn with AI and retain it, with fewer false accusations. *Assumes gains last beyond a few weeks, which is untested.*⁶
5. **Impact:** credentials still certify learning, fairly, for every student. *Assumes gaps in access and confidence narrow.*²

The weakest link is 3 to 4: the trials cited here ran from two lessons to six weeks, so lasting effects were not measured.⁶ ⁸

---

## Evidence from the field

**AI tutoring, Harvard physics.** A randomised crossover trial with 194 students found a carefully built AI tutor produced learning gains of 0.63 standard deviations over in-class active learning. The tutor relied on expert prompts and pre-written solutions, covered two lessons and measured no retention.⁶

**After-school AI tutoring, Edo State, Nigeria.** Six weeks of English tutoring with Microsoft Copilot raised scores by 0.31 standard deviations, most for girls and higher-attaining students. Floods, strikes and students' work disrupted attendance, and long-term effects are unknown.⁸

**The two-lane model, University of Sydney.** Supervised assessment of learning is used sparingly at key points; open assessment for learning invites AI use. It is a practice model, not an evaluated trial.⁹

---

## Who leads this work

The OECD, UNESCO, Australia's Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency, Jisc, the Centre for Research in Assessment and Digital Learning at Deakin University, and teaching and learning centres. Design joins where assessment tasks are written.

---

## What design cannot do alone

- **Detection.** Australia's higher education regulator advises that AI use appears almost impossible to detect.⁷ Assessment design has to carry validity instead.
- **Unequal access and confidence.** Gaps by gender and social grade need institutional provision of tools.² Held by institutions.
- **Evidence that ages.** Findings on specific tools date quickly.³ Held by researchers, through replication.

---

## Where effort counts for less, and for more

| Counts for less | Counts for more |
|---|---|
| The field is crowded with policy and regulatory activity. | Policy sets the frame. What would help teaching staff redesign the tasks themselves? |
| The evidence base is short-term. | Which design choices are worth testing over a full term or program? |
| Detection fails. | Securing a few key points across a program is more workable than securing every task. What would make that standard? |

---

## Open questions

- Do learning gains from AI tutoring last beyond a few weeks?
- Do results hold for higher-order tasks, not only structured content?
- Are AI-generated accessible formats, such as image descriptions, reliable without expert review? Early tests found failures on complex diagrams.¹⁰
