# Knowing whether it worked

How can organizations find out which programs change practice, and act on what they find?

---

## What solving it would make possible

Evaluation built in at design, measured by what people do afterwards, and returned to the evidence base. Part of the method is already known: how useful learners found training predicted transfer better than how much they enjoyed it.

---

## The three questions

Scale
- Organizations spend an estimated $399 billion a year on training worldwide. [verify: market estimate; confirm method]
- Across 162 studies, training produced medium-to-large effects at every level, from reactions to results. [verify study count]
- Across 139 government-funded teacher training programmes in 14 countries, most differed sharply from the features of programmes shown to raise learning, with fewer chances to practise and less follow-up.

What could be: knowing which programs change practice directs that spending to what works.

Neglectedness
- Few teacher training programmes worldwide are evaluated, and the results of those that are vary widely.
- Among 150 Canadian training professionals, organizations evaluated reactions most often and results least. Only behaviour and results evaluation were linked to transfer. [verify: read the article, not the summary]

What could be: the measures most linked to transfer are the least used, which is where design can act.

Solvability
- How much learners enjoyed training barely predicted transfer (r = .07); how useful they found it did better (r = .18).
- Supervisor support correlated with transfer at r = .31, and peer support at r = .14.

What could be: evaluation questions and supervisor roles can both be built in when a program is designed.

---

## Reading the figures

| Figure | Measures | Does not show |
|---|---|---|
| "Only 10% of training transfers" | An estimate offered by a practitioner in 1982, later repeated as a measurement | Any measurement of transfer |
| "54% evaluate behaviour" | Self-reports from 779 professionals in a 2019 survey; "uses" means at least sometimes | How often evaluation is done well, or for most programs |
| $399 billion | A market model built from industry research and economic data | A census of spending, or how much is wasted |
| 139 programmes in 14 countries | Features of government-funded teacher training at scale, compared with 33 rigorously evaluated programmes | Learning outcomes of the at-scale programmes themselves |

## Theory of change

1. Inputs: an evaluation plan agreed with managers before design begins. Assumes evaluation serves named users and their decisions.
2. Activities: ask how learners will use what they learned, then check practice at 60 to 90 days. Assumes usefulness ratings predict transfer better than enjoyment.
3. Outputs: contrasting cases of success and non-success, with the barriers each names. Assumes contrasting cases reveal what enables or blocks use.
4. Outcomes: managers close support gaps and the design is revised. Assumes supervisor support is linked to transfer.
5. Impact: more of what organizations spend on training becomes changed practice. Assumes findings are used.

The weakest link is 4 to 5: in the field examples below, the sources do not report whether evaluation went on to change the program.

---

## Evidence from the field

Coaching after training, a US public agency. Productivity among 31 managers rose 22% after three days of training, and 88% after eight weeks of coaching followed it. There was no control group, the lead author was also the instructor, and extra time on task is confounded with the coaching.

Success Case Method, coach training, South Africa. Of 55 people surveyed, 20 reported applying the training with good results. Barriers included personal circumstances and unmet expectations. All evidence was self-reported.

Two evaluation methods, one result, Puerto Rico. The Success Case Method judged two public health training programs highly successful; a traditional summative evaluation of the same results would have called them disappointing.

---

## Who leads this work

Training and organizational psychology researchers, professional associations for training and evaluation, practitioners of the Success Case Method, and evaluators working in the utilization-focused tradition. Design joins where evaluation questions are chosen.

---

## What design cannot do alone

- The work environment. Supervisor support, peer support and chances to practise are held by managers.
- Attribution. Even the Success Case Method does not claim training alone caused results.
- Publication bias. Reaction-only evaluations are rarely published, so research does not reflect everyday practice.

---

## Where effort counts for less, and for more

| Counts for less | Counts for more |
|---|---|
| Training already works on average. | Averages hide which programs work. What would let each organization find out about its own? |
| Much of what predicts transfer lies in the work environment. | Supervisors can be part of the design. What role would they hold before and after training? |
| Interventions before and after training show small effects on transfer. | Small effects compound across programs. Which ones are cheapest to build in? |

---

## Open questions

- Does evaluating behaviour cause better transfer, or do both mark organizations that take training seriously?
- What is the real rate of transfer? The widely quoted 10% figure was not a measurement.
- Are evaluation findings used at all?
- How far does this evidence hold elsewhere? Most of the training-evaluation studies cited here come from North America.
