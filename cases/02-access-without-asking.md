Watershed · Case 02 · Who can use it

# Access that does not depend on asking

How can learning work for disabled people without depending on a request?

---

## What solving it would make possible

Materials that disabled people can use on the same day as everyone else, without disclosing anything. Accommodation work concentrates on needs that are genuinely individual. Features built for some serve many: in one survey, students used captions to focus and retain information, though 19% reported hearing difficulties.⁸

---

## The three questions

**Scale**
- About 1.3 billion people, 16% of the world's population, live with significant disability.¹
- About one third of first-year Canadian university students in 2025 reported a disability or impairment, most commonly related to mental health.²

*What could be:* access built in once serves every learner who meets the resource.

**Neglectedness**
- In a US national study, about 35% of students who received disability services in high school disclosed their disability to their college.³ **[verify]**
- In 2026, 95.9% of the top million home pages had automatically detectable WCAG failures.⁴ **[verify]**

*What could be:* the gap sits where materials are made, which is where design acts.

**Solvability**
- Supports open to all students, such as tutoring and writing centres, were linked to persistence and completion for students with disabilities.³ **[verify]**
- Evidence for UDL as a whole framework is contested; the studies cited for it mostly support single techniques.⁵ ⁶

*What could be:* techniques with the strongest evidence become defaults.

## Reading the figures

| Figure | Measures | Does not show |
|---|---|---|
| 1.3 billion, 16% | A global estimate of significant disability¹ | A precise count |
| One third of first-year students | Self-report in a survey of over 11,000 students at 39 universities² | All Canadian students, or students at colleges |
| 35% disclosed | A US national cohort who received services in high school³ | Students never identified in school, or students today **[verify cohort years]** |
| 95.9% of home pages | Errors an automated tool detects on one home page per site⁴ | Problems that need human judgement |
| UDL effect sizes | Mostly one-group, before-and-after designs⁶ | That UDL caused the gain |

## Theory of change

1. **Inputs:** leadership by disabled people; WCAG, UDL and disability studies. *Assumes designers have time, mandate and budget.*
2. **Activities:** captions, transcripts, flexible formats and deadlines in the first draft. *Assumes learners' devices and assistive technology can use them.*
3. **Outputs:** resources people can use without asking. *Assumes those who need built-in features use them.*
4. **Outcomes:** more disabled learners take part and persist without needing to ask. *Assumes universal supports aid persistence.*³
5. **Impact:** learning reaches people it now misses, and accommodation covers the rest. *Assumes learners are in school and connected.*

The weakest link is 3 to 4: the sources reviewed here did not measure whether built-in access reduces requests.

---

## Evidence from the field

**Accessible digital textbooks, Nicaragua.** Prototypes with narration, sign language video and audio description were tested with 113 first- and second-grade students, 29 of them disabled, in three schools. Most students were engaged, and disabled students' independence grew. The prototypes worked least well for students with visual disabilities, most of whom had not yet been taught a screen reader. The authors note the sample is too small to generalise by disability type.⁷

**Captions and transcripts, United States.** A survey of 2,124 students at 15 universities found students used captions to focus, retain information and cope with poor audio, and used transcripts as study guides. Learning gains were not measured.⁸ **[verify]**

---

## Who leads this work

Disabled people and disability-led organisations, alongside accessibility advisors and accommodation professionals, CAST, the Inclusive Design Research Centre at OCAD University, and UNICEF's Accessible Digital Textbooks for All. Design joins where materials are made.

---

## What design cannot do alone

- **Individual accommodation.** Built-in access reduces how often it is needed and never replaces it. Held by accessibility advisors and accommodation offices.
- **Infrastructure.** Devices, connectivity and assistive technology training shaped outcomes in the field evidence.⁷ Held by institutions and ministries.
- **School access.** A resource cannot reach a child who is not in school. Children with disabilities are 49% more likely never to have attended school.⁹ **[verify]**

---

## Where effort counts for less, and for more

| Counts for less | Counts for more |
|---|---|
| Legislation already directs resources here. | Legislation sets a floor. What would make designing above it the default? |
| Design cannot reach children out of school. | Which partners hold school access, and how can design work alongside them? |
| UDL as a framework is contested. | Which single techniques have the strongest evidence, and how do they become standard? |

---

## Open questions

- Does access built in from the start reduce accommodation requests?
- Do features tested in small pilots hold across an institution's whole course inventory?
- Are AI-generated accessible formats reliable without expert review? Early tests found failures on complex STEM diagrams.¹⁰
